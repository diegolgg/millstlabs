# Hanabi wins the sandbox; Liar's Dice cross-checks it

Mill Street Labs should build its proof of concept on **two-player Hanabi**, with strategies transmitted as written convention documents plus the executable bots that implement them, and cross-check the organizational effect on **one small OpenSpiel imperfect-information game (one-die Liar's Dice or Leduc poker)**, where exact exploitability supplies an absolute, game-independent yardstick. Hanabi is the only candidate that scores well on all six criteria at once. Its canonical research metric, cross-play between independently trained groups, is literally a convention-transfer measurement: independent self-play seeds score **23.97 alone but 2.52 together** ([Other-Play](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979)). The real-world H-group conventions are a versioned, PR-governed written rulebook (levels 1–25) that independent teams have re-implemented as bots in NodeJS, Scala and Rust, so "teach a convention, verify it, adopt it" already has a human precedent ([hanabi.github.io](https://github.com/hanabi/hanabi.github.io); [hanabi-engine](https://github.com/Zamiell/hanabi-engine)). The engine runs at **0.1 ms per turn** and a JAX port reaches **5.0×10⁶ steps/s**, so once agents author bots the per-game cost is effectively zero ([Bard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.00506); [JaxMARL](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.10090)). A ladder of published anchors spans rule-based bots (22.99–24.89), humans (23.37), RL (24.48) and LLMs (**≤17.5 even with engine-supplied deductions**), plus a hosted human-proxy oracle that scores 1,000 games per submission ([AH2AC2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21490v2); [Sparks](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18077)). No published paper has put an LLM population, code transmission or teaching on Hanabi, so the result would be uncontested, and the design is fully orthogonal to the cofounder's grid-world/PPO/LoRA-inheritance sandbox (a card game, no gradients, artifacts that are text and code). Catan via Catanatron is the strongest competitive alternative because its strategies are already Python classes evaluated in thousands of games, but its only metric is a win rate that needs **1,000 games for ±3 points** ([HexMachina](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)). Werewolf has the deepest LLM-training literature and in-game team competition, but keeps the LLM in the per-turn loop at **$0.3–1 and 12–36 minutes per game** and cannot resolve win-rate differences below ~14 points at 100 games ([MafiaScope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10645); [ParliamentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28146)). OpenSpiel small games have the only absolute metric and the strongest 2026 program-evolution baselines but are neither visual nor "skillful." The white paper must name the counter-evidence: a September 2026 study found socially learning LLM populations **30–52% less efficient than solo learners at matched token budgets** ([Jha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.38516)), a June 2026 study found that in **87% of LLM program-evolution chains over 93% of mutations revisit an already-seen structure** ([Mutation Without Variation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05408)), and SwarmWorld found sharing improves portfolio resilience but lowers the best single artifact ([SwarmWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26081)).

## The LLM must leave the per-turn loop, and that halves the field

The most consequential finding across the six research notes is arithmetic. Every candidate in which the language model must speak or act on every turn tops out at tens to low hundreds of games per condition in the published literature, because each game costs real money and real minutes. Full-press Diplomacy runs at "over $100 without any retries" per game and one to 36 hours of wall-clock ([Every](https://every.to/p/how-we-made-ai-diplomacy-work)); Welfare Diplomacy games "summed to more than three million tokens" and the authors were "limited to only five games per experimental condition" ([arXiv 2310.08901](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.08901)). Civilization VI runs cost "$31–229 in API fees" and "2–8 hours" each, with only 23 admissible runs in the whole study ([CivBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02459v1)); even the cheapest 2026 CivRealm setup burns about **367k tokens per 150-turn game** with n=10 per method ([SAGA](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.29932v1)). Social deduction is an order of magnitude cheaper but still LLM-bound: 2,054 seven-player Among Us games cost "$2000 of OpenRouter API credits" ([Among Us sandbox](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04072)); a probed gpt-4o-mini Mafia game costs "a median $0.36 and 36 minutes" ([MafiaScope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10645)); an eight-player Werewolf game on a local A100 takes "approximately 12–13 minutes," with 100 games consuming roughly **21 GPU-hours** ([WOLF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09187)). At those rates one 1,000-game generation costs $300–1,000 or 200–600 serial machine-hours, and "thousands of generations" is simply out of reach. Even the most rigorous LLM-game evaluation ever run, Kaggle Game Arena, needed ~31,500 Werewolf games and 900,000 poker hands to rate just ten models ([Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31473v1)).

The games that survive are the ones in which the strategy is an artifact (a convention document, a bot, a policy program) that a fast simulator evaluates without calling the model. Hanabi's engine runs "around 0.1ms per turn on a CPU" and is "extremely lightweight" ([Bard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.00506)); JaxMARL measured the original Python-wrapped engine at 2.1×10³ steps/s and its own port at 5.0×10⁶ steps/s with 10,000 parallel environments ([JaxMARL](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.10090)). Catanatron advertises "thousands of games in the order of seconds" ([catanatron](https://github.com/bcollazo/catanatron)). OpenSpiel's C++ core supports a 50 ms-per-decision, 1,000-simulation MCTS budget in DeepMind's 2026 program-evolution paper ([arXiv 2609.33115](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33115)). In this regime the LLM is called only to author, teach, critique or revise an artifact, so spend scales with the number of teaching events rather than the number of games. That is exactly how HexMachina ran on Catan (20 Coder calls per phase, ten runs of 100 games each, "60 hours across two machines" that were both laptops) ([arXiv 2506.04651v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)) and how CodeClash ran across seven bot arenas with 1,000 simulations per round ([CodeClash](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00839v1)). The same logic explains why every LLM-Hanabi paper evaluates on 10–50 games per model while every RL-Hanabi paper evaluates on 1,000–10,000: the former keep the LLM in the loop, the latter do not ([Sparks](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18077); [OBL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.04000)).

The orthogonality constraint removes a second group. The cofounder's sandbox is a grid world with independent PPO, a small-LLM feature extractor carrying per-agent LoRA weights, reproduction with policy inheritance, and a shared knowledge corpus. Any candidate that is itself a spatial world with gradient-trained agents (Overcooked, Melting Pot's commons substrates, Neural MMO, Craftax-MA, Lux S3, Generals.io) would make the two sandboxes look like variants of one experiment rather than two independent tests of one organizational thesis. Overcooked is the painful exclusion: it has the richest population-training literature (FCP, MEP, HSP, COLE through the ZSC-Eval toolkit) and the best visualizer, but an ICLR 2025 paper showed its cross-play failures "can largely be attributed to poor state-coverage rather than more sophisticated coordination challenges" and concluded the classic layouts are "not suitable as a ZSC benchmark" ([OvercookedV2](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17821); [ZSC-Eval](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05208)). Melting Pot and Neural MMO have no LLM-program-evolution baselines, and their scores are normalized against trained exploiters or task completion, which is substrate-specific ([Melting Pot 2.0](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13746); [NMMO 2.0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03736)). Generals.io has a 50.7M-steps/s JAX simulator and a live human ladder, but Softmax (Metta-AI) already runs 154+ live bot leagues including Generals and a Battlecode 2026 port, with an "MMR with rating-based matchmaking" and a coding-agent loop, so the platform itself is not a differentiator ([arXiv 2606.23348](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23348v1); [softmax.com](https://softmax.com)). What remains is a short list: Hanabi, Catan, Werewolf (kept as the strongest LLM-in-the-loop option, to be honest about the trade) and OpenSpiel's small imperfect-information games.

The sample-size arithmetic sharpens the same ranking. Hanabi's per-game standard deviation back-solved from published standard errors is ~1.4 for strong RL agents (24.48±0.02 over 5,000 games) and ~3.5 for Rainbow-level agents, implying roughly **30 to 190 games for a ±0.5-point 95% CI**, while a bimodal bomb-out distribution typical of weak agents (σ≈8, from the Other-Play human study's s.e.m. of 1.23 over 40 games) needs ~1,000 ([arXiv 2511.22581](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.22581v1); [Bard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.00506); [Other-Play](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979)). Catan's best scripted bot wins **51.0% [48%, 54%]** in a four-seat matchup and the evolved HexMachina 54.1% [51%, 57%], so 1,000 games buy ±3 points and only effects of five points or more are reliably detectable per teaching event ([arXiv 2506.04651v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)). In Werewolf, the Game Arena rule-balance study needed 500 self-play games for a ±4-point CI, and ParliamentBench states that "at n = 100 per model... the paired design cannot reliably resolve overall win-rate differences below roughly 14 percentage points" ([Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31473); [ParliamentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28146)). For Elo-style pairwise ratings the forum-derived error formula implies ~190 games per pair for ±50 Elo and ~740 for ±25 (a secondary source, flagged in the unverified table below) ([TalkChess](https://talkchess.com/forum3/viewtopic.php?t=57969)). The practical consequence: a population experiment that wants per-generation, per-group measurements at ±0.5 points needs hundreds of games per measurement, which is affordable only when games are free.

### Option A: Hanabi, with conventions as text and bots as code

**Criterion 1, thesis fit.** Hanabi maps onto every mechanism in the thesis without inventing anything. Groups are convention lineages; the lock-in that makes independently trained groups incompatible is the best-documented phenomenon in the game's literature, and it is measured by a protocol the field already treats as standard: "ZSC performance is evaluated through cross-play, where agents of the same learning algorithm come from independent training runs" ([R3D2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14555)). Self-play SAD scores 23.97±0.04 but cross-play collapses to 2.52±0.34, and the published ladder of fixes (Other-Play 22.07, OBL-L4 23.76, R3D2 ~22–23, entropy-regularised IPPO 24.45 inter-seed cross-play) gives a new population method clean numbers to beat ([Other-Play](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979); [OBL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.04000); [arXiv 2511.22581](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.22581v1)). Teaching has a real-world precedent at both fidelity levels the thesis names: the H-group conventions are a written rulebook whose changes go through pull requests and are "voted in," and at least four independent teams have turned that prose into bots (will-hanabi-bot in NodeJS, scala-bot, raikan, Zamiell's Rust hanabi-engine encoding "cumulative H-Group profiles from Level 1 through Level 25") ([hanabi.github.io](https://github.com/hanabi/hanabi.github.io); [will-hanabi-bot](https://github.com/will-hanabi-bot/hanabi-bot/blob/master/README.md); [hanabi-engine](https://github.com/Zamiell/hanabi-engine)). Verification before adoption is operational: run the taught bot for N self-play games, then cross-play it against the group's incumbent bot, both at engine speed. Credit assignment and counterfactuals are cheap because the engine is deterministic given a seed, so a student's score with and without a teacher's artifact can be compared on identical deals (this paired design is an inference from the engine's determinism, not a published protocol). The one honest gap is that Hanabi is purely cooperative, so "groups compete for compute" must be imposed at the population level rather than arising in-game. That is a feature for a proof of concept, because it isolates the organizational mechanism from kingmaking, role luck and the non-transitive cycles that plague competitive games; the cross-check game covers the adversarial case.

**Criterion 2, API and speed.** The Hanabi Learning Environment is a C++ core with Gym-like Python bindings under Apache-2.0, but the repository was archived read-only on April 18, 2024 and ships no renderer, so Mill Street should vendor it or use the JaxMARL port ([HLE repo](https://github.com/google-deepmind/hanabi-learning-environment); [JaxMARL](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.10090)). Two actively maintained alternatives already expose the game to exactly the kind of agents the thesis needs: Zamiell's hanabi-engine (Rust, "deterministic game logic and player-safe observations," connects bots to hanab.live, replay analysis with `--explain` and `--format json`) and Metta-AI's cogame-hanabi (MIT, Claude-API LLM agents plus two scripted baselines, policies as either prompts or Nim code, a WebAssembly replay viewer) ([hanabi-engine](https://github.com/Zamiell/hanabi-engine); [cogame-hanabi](https://github.com/Metta-AI/cogame-hanabi)). OpenSpiel also lists hanabi as "thoroughly tested," which matters for the cross-check design below ([OpenSpiel games](https://openspiel.readthedocs.io/en/latest/games.html)).

**Criterion 3, long runs.** With 0.1 ms per turn and roughly 50–70 turns per two-player game, the engine sustains on the order of hundreds of games per second per core; even through the Python layer at 2.1×10³ steps/s it manages ~30 games/s. A generation in which twenty agents each evaluate a bot over 1,000 games is minutes of CPU, and the JAX port makes a frozen-ladder cross-play matrix of hundreds of bot pairs a matter of seconds ([Bard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.00506); [JaxMARL](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.10090)). LLM spend is confined to authoring and teaching events. The caveat is that no paper reports dollar or token cost for LLM Hanabi play, and if Mill Street chooses to let agents play directly rather than through bots, the design inherits the 10-games-per-configuration ceiling of the LLM literature ([Sparks](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18077)).

**Criterion 4, baselines.** The anchor ladder is the deepest of any candidate. Rule-based: SmartBot 22.99 (2p), FireFlower 22.56, and the hat-guessing WTFWThat 24.89 (5p, 91.5% perfect games) ([Bard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.00506)). Humans: hanab.live H-group means of 23.37 (2p) and 23.25 (3p) from a released set of 3,079 games ([AH2AC2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21490v2)). RL: BAD 23.92, OBL-L4 24.10 self-play / 23.76 cross-play, entropy-IPPO 24.48 / 24.45 ([OBL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.04000); [arXiv 2511.22581](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.22581v1)). LLMs across five independent benchmarks: o3 17.5 with engine deductions and 15.9 without, GPT-4-turbo 13.33, non-reasoning models below 10, and "none [of the LLMs] approach even the first quartile of human scores" ([Sparks](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18077); [LLM-Coordination](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.03903v3); [SPIN-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.12349)). Uniquely, AH2AC2 hosts human-proxy agents (KL-regularised IPPO trained on 101,096 two-player and 46,525 three-player hanab.live games) and gives each submission 1,000 evaluation games per setting through an API, with a leaderboard where OBL scores 21.04 zero-shot; this is an external, frozen oracle for "did the population converge to human-compatible conventions" ([AH2AC2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21490v2)). For organization-level baselines, every open evolutionary harness needs the same thing, an initial program with mutable blocks and an `evaluate()` returning scalar metrics, so a Hanabi bot evaluator is a drop-in for ShinkaEvolve (islands with migration, Apache-2.0), OpenEvolve (MAP-Elites plus ring-migration islands), GEPA (Pareto pool, accept-only-if-improved), HGM (clade-metaproductivity credit, open code), and Economy of Minds (market selection, open code) ([ShinkaEvolve](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19349v1); [OpenEvolve](https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve); [GEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457); [HGM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21614v1); [EoM](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02859v1)). The niche is empty: no published work was found in which LLMs write Hanabi bots as code or evolve conventions in a population; the nearest pieces are cogame-hanabi's "a policy is just a prompt" testbed and Ashery et al.'s naming-game conventions ([cogame-hanabi](https://github.com/Metta-AI/cogame-hanabi); [Science Advances 2025](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adu9368)).

**Criterion 5, metric.** The score is absolute (0–25) and portable across player counts; cross-play is a non-game-specific protocol ("same algorithm, independent runs, play together, N games"); BR-Prox from ZSC-Eval normalizes ego performance by approximate best-response performance per partner and correlated 0.90–1.00 with human rankings ([ZSC-Eval](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05208)). Required sample sizes range from ~30 games per measurement for low-variance code bots to ~1,000 for bomb-prone agents, as computed above. The one wrinkle is the interpretability trade-off at the top of the ladder: hat-guessing bots reach 24.89 at five players only because "all players follow the same algorithm," and the same bot drops to 19.45 at two players and near zero with partners who do not share it ([Eger](https://gruss.cc/files/waitasecond.pdf); [Bard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.00506)). A population that converges to hat-guessing would score superbly in self-play and terribly on AH2AC2, which is precisely why both must be reported.

**Criterion 6, interpretability.** A two-player game is ~60 discrete turns, auditable in a hanab.live replay, and hanabi-engine's `--explain` annotates each decision with the convention that produced it; cogame-hanabi ships a static WebAssembly replay viewer ([hanabi-engine](https://github.com/Zamiell/hanabi-engine); [cogame-hanabi](https://github.com/Metta-AI/cogame-hanabi)). A human can read the convention document a group adopted, watch the bot apply it, and see where cross-play broke down. The gap is that HLE has no renderer and no shared replay JSON schema across HLE, hanab.live and Metta was confirmed.

### Option B: Catan via Catanatron, with strategies as Python players

**Criterion 1.** Catanatron is the only candidate where a published protocol already treats an LLM-written strategy as a self-contained, testable artifact: HexMachina stores evolved strategies "as executable Python classes inheriting from a Player interface" and used "fixed seeds" in "deterministic experiments" to isolate genuine improvements, which is the counterfactual machinery the thesis needs ([arXiv 2506.04651v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)). Player-to-player trading is implemented at engine level as structured actions (`OFFER_TRADE`, `ACCEPT_TRADE`, `CONFIRM_TRADE`), so bargaining exists without natural language ([catanatron enums](https://github.com/bcollazo/catanatron/blob/master/catanatron/catanatron/models/enums.py)). But group competition is weak: four-player free-for-all with dice, kingmaking and no team structure, and nobody has verified that the default bots ever issue trade offers, so trade-based group dynamics would have to be designed in.

**Criterion 2.** Pip-installable, GPL-3.0, Gymnasium environment, CLI batch play with JSON output, a React web UI "to watch, inspect, and play games," and an active repository (last push 2026-09-16) ([catanatron](https://github.com/bcollazo/catanatron); [GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/bcollazo/catanatron)). The "thousands of games per minute" claim is for fast bots; a random playout takes ~0.3 s, and HexMachina capped AlphaBeta at a "20 s decision cap," so throughput with strong bots on all four seats is unverified and likely much lower ([RESULTS_LOG](https://github.com/bcollazo/catanatron/blob/master/docs/RESULTS_LOG.md); [arXiv 2506.04651v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)).

**Criterion 3.** The entire HexMachina study, including 10×100-game evaluations, ran in 60 hours on two laptops, so thousands of generations are affordable in compute; the binding cost is variance, since each strategy needs 500–1,000 games against frozen bots to be scored to ±3 points ([arXiv 2506.04651v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)).

**Criterion 4.** Thin. The only LLM-Catan paper is single-lineage, was withdrawn from ICLR 2026, and states "we release all code" without a URL in the text; a third-party reproduction repository exists ([OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=V0Fb4pwhS4); [arXiv 2506.04651v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)). Built-in bot win rates (AlphaBeta 80% vs ValueFunction, ValueFunction 90% vs GreedyPlayouts) are measured on 25 games ([docs](https://docs.catanatron.com/advanced/making-catanatron-stronger)). There is no cross-suite comparability: Catan is in TAG/PyTAG but in none of the LLM arenas ([PyTAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18123v1)). Evolve-harness baselines are a drop-in, which is the one strength here.

**Criterion 5.** Win rate against a frozen AlphaBeta is relative and seat-dependent; HexMachina's own table lists both the evolved player and AlphaBeta above 50%, and the exact seat composition was not extracted, so the numbers compare only within the paper's protocol ([arXiv 2506.04651v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04651v2)). No equilibrium or human anchor exists.

**Criterion 6.** Strong: the web UI replays games, and evolved strategies are code whose diffs a human can read. This is Catan's best argument, and the reason it stays on the shortlist as a competitive showcase if a visual multi-player game is ever needed.

### Option C: Werewolf, with playbooks as text

**Criterion 1.** Werewolf has the most natural in-game group competition (werewolves vs village) and the thesis's text-transmission primitive already works: SPaRTan has an agent play 30 self-play games, "write a first-person strategic playbook covering deception, detection, persuasion," prepend it to the system prompt, and transfer it across Werewolves, Spyfall and Resistance, including "strong-to-weak distillation into 6 student models"; the deceptive-side win rate rose from 24% to 36–43% but "the bulk of the gain arrives at R1... additional rounds redistribute rather than accumulate" ([Social Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09128)). Strategist did the same on Avalon with "round-robin games among the top ten strategies" ([Strategist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10635)). Population-based training exists (Xu et al.'s six LLM playstyles plus RL checkpoints; Thinker's fictitious self-play over the last 500 checkpoints) ([arXiv 2310.18940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18940); [arXiv 2402.02330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02330)). But verification of a taught playbook requires full LLM-played games, role luck dominates outcomes (villagers "win a mere 1.2% of 100,000 simulated games" without communication; the standard 8-player ruleset is Seer-Doctor exploitable at 73.4% villager win), and no cultural-evolution or teaching-credit work exists on any deduction game ([Werewolf Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.13943v1); [Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31473)).

**Criterion 2.** Engines are plentiful but uneven: Google's Werewolf Arena was archived read-only in September 2025; the Game Arena harness is Apache-2.0 with full trajectories; WereWolf-Plus and the 2023 Xu code are open; TextArena's SecretMafia and Social Gym add more ([werewolf_arena](https://github.com/google/werewolf_arena); [game_arena](https://github.com/google-deepmind/game_arena)). None removes the LLM from the loop.

**Criterion 3.** This is the disqualifier for long runs: $0.25–1 and 12–36 minutes per game with API models, 21 A100-hours per 100 games locally, and ~17K tokens per six-player Secret Mafia game or ~62K completion tokens per five-player Secret Hitler game ([Among Us sandbox](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04072); [WOLF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.09187); [MindGames](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29512); [ParliamentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28146)).

**Criterion 4.** The richest trained-LLM stack of any game: RL-over-LLM (Xu, ICML 2024), latent-space self-play (LSPO, ICML 2025: 0.73/0.27 werewolf/village vs ReAct 0.58/0.16), MaKTO (NeurIPS 2025, 61% over 100 games), Stackelberg Speaker (ICLR 2026, village win 29.1%→84.2% over LSPO), and the Game Arena round-robin of ten frontier models ([LSPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04686); [MaKTO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14225); [arXiv 2510.09087](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09087); [Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31473)). Yet MaKTO and Xu et al. have no located code release, and the strong baselines are fine-tuned weights, which overlaps the cofounder's approach rather than complementing it.

**Criterion 5.** Game Arena's game-theoretic evaluation decomposes skill into role-specific contributions with bootstrap CIs because "raw win rates conflate individual skill with role-assignment luck"; Among Us has Deception/Detection Elo with 1,000-sample bootstrap CIs ([Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31473); [Among Us sandbox](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04072)). But the 14-point floor at n=100, opponent-unstable win rates (Kendall τ of −0.33 across opponent swaps), and format noise that "skewed" the MindGames Secret Mafia track (50.3% error games) make small organizational effects undetectable at affordable sample sizes ([ParliamentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28146); [MindGames](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29512)).

**Criterion 6.** MafiaScope's D3 viewer shows per-utterance belief probes with "fork-and-replay" of any state, the closest existing instrument for "see why one population is better," but everything else is transcripts ([MafiaScope](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10645)).

### Option D: OpenSpiel Liar's Dice or Leduc poker, with policies as programs

**Criterion 1.** Strategies are programs and verification is an exact computation: NashConv is "the sum of how much each player gains by deviating to their best response," and exploitability is tabular for games of this size (Kuhn 12 information sets, Leduc 288, one-die Liar's Dice 24,576; counts from a search snippet, flagged below) ([PSRO](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2017/file/3323fe11e9595c09af38fe67567a9394-Paper.pdf); [arXiv 2004.09677](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.09677)). PSRO, CFR variants, α-rank and VasE ship in the library, so the frozen-ladder and Nash-clustering analyses are off the shelf ([OpenSpiel algorithms](https://openspiel.readthedocs.io/en/latest/algorithms.html)). Group competition is adversarial by construction, which is what makes this the right cross-check: Czarnecki et al. show real games have "a strong transitive component" plus non-transitive cycles that diminish with skill, and a population must be at least as wide as the widest cycle layer to converge ([Czarnecki 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.09468)). What is missing is any natural "convention" layer; a taught artifact here is a policy or search heuristic, not a shared language.

**Criterion 2.** OpenSpiel 2.0 (July 14, 2026) adds typed, JSON-interchangeable state/action/observation structs so states can be sent "to a frontend, an LLM, or a database," claims 120+ games, and says its design "was inspired by Kaggle Game Arena use cases, such as language models interacting with game environments" ([OpenSpiel 2.0](https://mlanctot.info/introducing_open_spiel_2.0/)). No games-per-second benchmark for the C++ core was found, but the 2026 DeepMind budget of 50 ms per decision at 1,000 simulations implies ~20k simulations/s ([arXiv 2609.33115](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33115)).

**Criterion 3.** Trivially cheap; the only cost is LLM authoring.

**Criterion 4.** The strongest program-evolution SOTA of any candidate: DeepMind's AlphaEvolve discovered VAD-CFR and SHOR-PSRO on 3-player Kuhn, Leduc, 4-card Goofspiel and 5-sided Liar's Dice, matching or beating SOTA in 10 of 11 games with exploitability below 1e-3 ([alphaXiv 2602.16928](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.16928v2)); the September 2026 follow-up evolved C++ MCTS mechanisms across 50 OpenSpiel games with Soft Condorcet Optimization ratings (509.34±0.39 vs PUCT 503.92±1.43), α-rank, pairwise win rates, anti-cheating gates, code-complexity bounds and "stochastic quorum scheduling" to control evaluation noise ([arXiv 2609.33115](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33115); [SCO](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2025/pdfs/p1253.pdf)). LLM-acting baselines exist in GTBench (Kuhn, Liar's Dice, with MCTS-1000 opponents), TextArena and Board Game Arena, and self-play RL baselines in SPIRAL and MARSHAL (Kuhn poker, 8×H100, ~25 h) ([GTBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12348); [SPIRAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.24119); [MARSHAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15414v3)).

**Criterion 5.** The only candidate with an absolute distance-to-optimal metric, plus SCO and α-rank for relative ratings that handle cycles. Nothing else on the list can say "this population is 3×10⁻³ from unexploitable."

**Criterion 6.** The weakest: no replay viewer, and "why population A is better" would be shown through policy tables or exploitability-by-infoset rather than a watchable game. The games are also small enough that reviewers may call them toy problems; SPIRAL's authors used them precisely because they are small ([SPIRAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.24119)).

## Hanabi's cross-play collapse is the null hypothesis the mechanism must beat

The deepest reason to choose Hanabi is that the thesis's central claim already has a precise negative control in the literature. Independently trained Hanabi groups do not merely fail to help each other; they actively break each other, because each converges on private signalling conventions. Self-play SAD scores 23.97 but cross-play 2.52, and against a human-clone bot 0.83 ([OBL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.04000)). Mill Street's organizational claim is that groups which teach transmissible conventions, verify them before adoption, migrate members and credit teachers will converge on conventions that are both high-scoring within the group and compatible across groups. The measurement is already standard: a within-group versus between-group cross-play matrix over frozen generations, which is the Other-Play protocol with population structure added. If the mechanism works, between-group cross-play rises toward self-play over generations while the no-teaching ablation stays near the SAD collapse; if it fails, the figure shows it just as clearly.

The second reason is that gains in Hanabi come from exactly where the thesis says they should: from code and conventions, not from better prompting. Across the LLM-Hanabi literature the largest levers are engine-side deduction helpers, which raise o3 from 15.9 to 17.5 and cost other models 1.2–3.7 points when removed, and RL fine-tuning, which lifts Qwen3-4B from 4.8 to 12.3 ([Sparks](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18077)). Human conventions added to the RL action space raised Rainbow's five-player score from 15.26 to 19.05 and cross-play to 17.69 with "below 30 million" training steps against 20 billion for ACHA ([arXiv 2412.06333](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.06333)). In Overcooked, distilling LLM reasoning into "an executable policy tree" gave +35.4% reward with 77.7% fewer LLM queries and 97.1% lower latency ([Co-pi-tree](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08596)). A population whose agents write deduction helpers and convention bots that other agents adopt is therefore working on the lever that actually moves the score, and it sidesteps the LLM theory-of-mind weakness that caps direct play: LLMs show "marked difficulty with scenarios demanding active consideration of partners' beliefs and intentions," and first-order ToM correlates with score at r=0.76 ([LLM-Coordination](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.03903v3); [LLM-Hanabi](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04980)). Writing a convention bot is a coding task, and that is where frontier models are strong; human teams did it in a few thousand lines of NodeJS or Scala.

Mapping the five mechanisms concretely: a group is a lineage holding a convention document and a bot repository; a teaching event is one agent sending another a convention delta plus the code that implements it; verification is the student running the candidate for N seeded self-play games and then cross-playing it with the group's incumbent before merging; credit is the student's paired-seed score delta attributed back through the teaching lineage, discounted the way HGM's clade-metaproductivity credits descendants' successes to ancestors ([HGM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21614v1)); migration moves an agent and its artifacts between lineages, and the receiving group's verification step is what prevents the convention-pollution that Ashery et al. showed when "committed adversarial minorities can flip conventions" ([Science Advances 2025](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adu9368)); competition for compute allocates the next generation's LLM-call budget across groups in proportion to relative population performance at Nash, Balduzzi's v(P,Q), rather than raw mean, so a group cannot inflate its share by cloning its best bot ([Balduzzi 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.08106); [Nash averaging](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.02643)). Economy of Minds is the closest published analogue to that last step, with "bucket-brigade" backward payments and wealth-driven reproduction, and it is open code, which makes it the natural market-based baseline ([EoM](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02859v1)).

Two-player is the right starting configuration because it has the most anchors (every RL paper, every LLM paper, the human dataset and the AH2AC2 proxies), the lowest variance, and the shortest logs; three-player is the right generalization test because AH2AC2 hosts 3p proxies and R3D2 reports zero-shot transfer across player counts ([AH2AC2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21490v2); [R3D2](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14555v1)). Reduced variants (Mini, Tiny, 3-colour/3-rank Hanabi) exist for cheap early generations if authoring budgets are tight, with full Hanabi reserved for evaluation ([MARS](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15414v1); [arXiv 2602.21515](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.21515)). One competitive signal deserves a flag: Metta-AI already built cogame-hanabi with Claude agents, scripted conventions as experimental conditions, and a replay viewer. That is a resource to reuse, not a reason to avoid the game, but it means the differentiator in the white paper has to be the population mechanism and its measured effect, not the testbed ([cogame-hanabi](https://github.com/Metta-AI/cogame-hanabi)).

## The counter-evidence the white paper has to answer

Three 2026 results cut directly against a naive "sharing helps" story and should be named in the paper's related-work and framed as the hypotheses the design is built to test. The most pointed is Jha, Kleiman-Weiner and Jaques (September 2026): 3–10 self-interested agents exchanging revisable skill files under an explicit per-round token budget were **30–52% less efficient than solo learners on a 25-arm bandit** (10 agents, 100 rounds, 4,096 tokens per round), and IFBench gains at matched cost were +0.14±0.35 and +0.98±0.85 points, not significant, while an algorithmic hierarchical-UCB population did gain ([Jha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.38516)). That paper has no verification step, no credit assignment and no group structure ("lack detailed provenance tracking beyond skill lineage"), which is exactly Mill Street's ablation ladder; the white paper's claim should be that verification and credit are what flip the sign, and the no-verification/no-credit ablations are the direct replication. The second is "Mutation Without Variation": in LLM-driven program evolution "in 87% of chains, over 93% of mutations revisit a previously seen structural form," a systematic homogeneity that classical genetic programming does not show ([arXiv 2606.05408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05408)). Group structure with migration is the standard evolutionary remedy, and ShinkaEvolve's islands plus novelty rejection by embeddings and an LLM judge is the baseline that already tries it, so the diversity metrics below (effective diversity, Nash-cluster count) must be reported alongside score. The third is SwarmWorld: shared worlds beat isolated search on held-out resilience (0.0446 vs 0.0356) but isolated search kept the strongest single artifact (0.3488 vs 0.2380), and ~95% of first reuse happened through observation rather than explicit transmission ([SwarmWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26081)). The honest framing is that the mechanism is expected to raise the population's Nash value and cross-group compatibility, not necessarily its single best bot, and both should be plotted.

The cultural-evolution precedents are also fragile. Vallinder and Hughes' donor game (12 agents, 10 generations, five runs, SEM shading and no formal tests) found that only Claude 3.5 Sonnet societies improved across generations, with "sensitive dependence on initial conditions" ([arXiv 2412.10270](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10270)); Horibe found robustness to free-riders varying more than tenfold across four backends ([arXiv 2608.04507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04507)); and the Rahwan-lab group-selection study found GPT-5.4 "preemptively and gradually" adapting when told the selection rule, a reminder that agents can game the organizational mechanism itself ([arXiv 2606.23343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23343)). On the harness side, a 2026 study with more than 3.1 million rollouts over 12 model-problem pairs found "variants of OpenEvolve generally underperform simpler alternatives," that no harness is universally superior, and that adaptive allocation across harnesses beats committing to one; it released null distributions and treats harness choice as a hyperparameter ([arXiv 2607.18235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18235)). CodeClash, the best LLM-writes-the-bot baseline, found "ungrounded edits in >65% of rounds," near-linear codebase bloat, and that "top models lose every round against expert human programmers" ([CodeClash](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.00839v1)). Sarkar et al. showed that co-trained Among Us crewmates "come up with simple strategies that result in high win rates but are easy to overcome with a more intelligent imposter," fixed only by freezing a teammate ([arXiv 2502.06060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06060)); in Hanabi the equivalent degenerate attractor is hat-guessing, and the AH2AC2 proxies are the frozen teammate.

Finally, there are cost and evaluation cautions. No paper reports dollar or token cost per Hanabi game for LLM agents; DGM's ~$22,000 and two weeks per SWE-bench run comes from a secondary summary, and HGM reports single runs with no seeds ([Gonzo ML](https://gonzoml.substack.com/p/darwin-godel-machine); [HGM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21614v1)). Elo should not be the primary rating: on LLM head-to-head data it "even with >> 1,000 permutations, struggles to achieve stable rankings," and Bradley-Terry with bootstrap CIs preserved transitivity better (77.29% vs 68.24%) ([Ranking Unraveled](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.14483)). And rankings disagree across games in every multi-game suite examined, which Game Arena states as "model strength is sharply stratified within each game environment, but not consistent across games" ([Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31473v1)); that is the argument for a cross-check game, and the reason not to claim generality from Hanabi alone.

## A game-agnostic evaluation suite and the headline figure

The suite should be built so that every metric is defined on a payoff matrix or an absolute score and can be computed for any game, with Hanabi and the OpenSpiel cross-check as its first two instances. The unit of analysis is the population run (seed), not the game: Agarwal et al. show percentile bootstrap CIs on the interquartile mean are reliable "for as few as N=10 runs" while medians need 50–100, so the design is **at least 10 population seeds per condition**, stratified bootstrap over seeds, IQM as the point estimate, and performance profiles rather than single aggregates ([rliable](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.13264)). That alone would exceed the reporting standard of every game-specific population paper in the notes (HGM and PopuLoRA single-run, Vallinder and Hughes five runs, Colas 2026 five searches) and match the harness study's repeated-trial protocol ([arXiv 2607.18235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18235)).

Within each seed, the core instrument is a **frozen ladder**: every generation's artifacts are checkpointed, and the current population is cross-played against every frozen generation and against a fixed anchor set, following AlphaStar's recipe of evaluating "against the entire league and the elite built-in bot (whose Elo is set to 0)" and checking that the league Nash "assigns small probabilities to players from previous iterations," which is the non-cycling test ([AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). From the resulting payoff matrix come four game-agnostic numbers per generation: relative population performance v(P_t, anchor) at Nash and effective diversity d(P) ([Balduzzi 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.08106)); the Nash-cluster count as the intransitivity measure ([Czarnecki 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.09468)); a Bradley-Terry rating of the population's Nash mixture against the anchors with 1,000-resample bootstrap CIs, the Chatbot Arena procedure, using ≥40 colour- or seat-balanced games per pair as Game Arena does for chess ([Chatbot Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04132); [Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31473v1)); and an ANNECS-style count of innovations that beat the archive and are later retained, borrowed from Enhanced POET, which is the right open-endedness proxy for a thousands-of-generations run ([Enhanced POET](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/wang20l/wang20l.pdf)). For aggregation across games, Nash averaging or VasE rather than the arithmetic mean, so no single game dominates and redundant anchors cannot inflate the score ([Nash averaging](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.02643); [VasE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03121)).

Game-specific instantiations stay thin. For Hanabi the anchor set is random, SmartBot, an OBL or entropy-IPPO checkpoint, will-hanabi-bot or hanabi-engine at a fixed H-group level, and the AH2AC2 human proxies via their 1,000-game API; the two headline quantities are within-group and between-group cross-play (0–25) with BR-Prox as the partner-normalized variant ([AH2AC2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21490v2); [ZSC-Eval](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.05208)). Measurement sizes follow the σ arithmetic above: ~50 seeded games per bot pair when bots are deterministic and low-variance, scaled to several hundred if bomb-out rates are high, with paired seeds across conditions to cut variance further. For the OpenSpiel cross-check the anchors are a CFR+ policy, the 50 ms PUCT reference and, if reproducible, VAD-CFR; the headline quantity is exact exploitability per generation plus SCO and α-rank, directly comparable to the two 2026 DeepMind papers ([arXiv 2609.33115](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33115); [alphaXiv 2602.16928](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.16928v2)). OpenSpiel 2.0 can host both games under one JSON state interface, with HLE or JaxMARL used only as the fast evaluation backend for Hanabi ([OpenSpiel 2.0](https://mlanctot.info/introducing_open_spiel_2.0/)).

Credit for students' improvement has no published metric, so it must be defined in-house and declared as such. The defensible definition is counterfactual and paired: for each teaching event, the student's anchor-set score on identical seeds before and after adopting the artifact, attributed to the teacher and propagated up the lineage with the decay HGM uses for clade-metaproductivity; PopuLoRA's teacher reward (1 minus student solve rate) credits difficulty rather than improvement and is the nearest contrast ([HGM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21614v1); [PopuLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.16727)). Because the engine is deterministic, this costs engine time only. Cost reporting is the other in-house standard: every curve plotted against cumulative LLM tokens and dollars, separately for authoring, teaching and verification calls, the matched-budget discipline of Jha et al. ([Jha et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.38516)).

The baseline set at matched LLM-call budget is ShinkaEvolve, OpenEvolve, GEPA, HGM and Economy of Minds, all open code and all needing only an evaluator that plays games and returns a score ([ShinkaEvolve](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19349v1); [OpenEvolve](https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve); [GEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457); [HGM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21614v1); [EoM](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02859v1)), plus a Vallinder-and-Hughes-style generational prompt-transmission baseline as the "cultural evolution without verification or credit" control ([arXiv 2412.10270](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10270)), and PBT as a conceptual compute-competition control if any fine-tuning is ever added ([PBT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.09846)). DGM is reproducible but ~4× HGM's cost for worse results and is not worth running ([HGM](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.21614v1)). The ablation ladder is the scientific core: full mechanism, minus teaching, minus verification, minus migration, minus credit, minus group competition (uniform compute), each with 10 seeds.

The headline figure for the white paper is a two-panel plot. Panel A: Hanabi between-group cross-play score (0–25, IQM over 10 seeds, stratified-bootstrap 95% bands) against cumulative LLM tokens, with one line per condition (full mechanism, five ablations, five harness baselines, generational-prompt control) and dashed horizontal anchors at OBL cross-play 23.76, hanab.live humans 23.37, SmartBot 22.99 and o3-with-deductions 17.5; a thin companion line shows self-play so the reader can see whether between-group compatibility tracks or lags it. Panel B: the same conditions on the OpenSpiel game with exploitability on a log axis and CFR+ and VAD-CFR anchors. The headline table for the one-pager is a single row per condition at matched budget, with final between-group cross-play, AH2AC2 proxy score, exploitability on the cross-check, Nash-cluster count, retained innovations and dollars spent, each with its CI, so that the organizational claim, the human-compatibility claim, the adversarial-robustness claim, and the diversity claim appear side by side and the Jha et al. and SwarmWorld objections are answered on the same page.

## Conclusion

The choice turns on one structural fact: the only way to run thousands of generations of an LLM population on a skillful game is to make the strategy an artifact that a simulator evaluates for free, and Hanabi is the game where that artifact, a written convention plus the bot that implements it, is also the thing the research community already measures with cross-play and already has a human precedent for in the H-group ecosystem. That alignment is rarer than it looks: Catan has the code artifact but not the metric, Werewolf has the text artifact but not the cost structure, and OpenSpiel has the metric but not the artifact or the audience. Choosing Hanabi also turns the field's best-known failure, convention lock-in, into the experiment's null hypothesis, which is a far stronger position for a white paper than beating a leaderboard.

The design implication is that Mill Street's contribution should be framed as organizational, not as a Hanabi agent. The 2026 counter-evidence says social learning among LLM agents is net-negative at matched cost and that LLM mutation collapses diversity; the ablation ladder on a free-to-simulate game is the cheapest rigorous way to show which of verification, credit, migration and group competition changes that sign, and the OpenSpiel cross-check with exact exploitability is the cheapest way to show the answer is not a property of one cooperative card game. If both panels move together, the two orthogonal sandboxes, the cofounder's gradient-based grid world and this artifact-based card game, become independent replications of one thesis rather than two demos.
